# 05 --- Construction PM Decision & Recovery Playbook

**Project:** Corporate Operations Center\
**Facility:** 75,000 SF Ground-Up Corporate Operations Center\
**Baseline Budget:** \$20.6M\
**Status Date:** September 15, 2026\
**Role:** Construction Project Manager --- Portfolio Simulation\
**Document Purpose:** Demonstrate how project controls are converted
into management decisions, recovery actions, executive escalation, and
measurable outcomes.

> **Portfolio Disclosure:** This is a professional construction project
> management simulation created for portfolio and interview
> demonstration. It is not represented as paid construction employment.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Executive Management Scenario

At the September 15, 2026 status date, the Corporate Operations Center
is approximately **49.9% actually complete versus 51.7% planned**,
placing the project about **1.8 percentage points behind plan**. The
revised project budget is **\$21.057M**, while the working forecast at
completion is approximately **\$20.55M**, leaving forecast headroom but
requiring continued protection of contingency.

The project has entered a high-coordination stage. Structural work is
nearing completion, envelope activities are progressing, Area A MEP
rough-in is active, long-lead electrical equipment remains under
procurement watch, and interior framing depends on achieving dry-in and
clearing MEP constraints.

The PM's priority is therefore not simply to report that the project is
behind. The priority is to determine **why**, identify which activities
can affect final completion, assign recovery ownership, quantify cost
and risk exposure, establish decision deadlines, and verify whether the
recovery plan is working.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. PM Management Objectives

1.  Protect the weather-tight milestone and prevent envelope delay from
    cascading into interiors.
2.  Recover Area A MEP rough-in without creating quality or rework
    exposure.
3.  Maintain visibility on main electrical gear and other long-lead
    procurement.
4.  Preserve remaining contingency by challenging elective scope growth
    and unpriced exposure.
5.  Close RFIs and submittals before they become field constraints.
6.  Maintain zero recordable safety incidents while schedule recovery
    activities increase field intensity.
7.  Prevent schedule acceleration from reducing QA/QC standards.
8.  Maintain an executive decision log so unresolved owner or design
    decisions have clear deadlines.
9.  Begin closeout planning early enough to avoid a late turnover
    documentation backlog.
10. Maintain one source of truth across schedule, cost, procurement,
    changes, risks, and action items.

### Decision Standard

A decision is escalated when at least one of the following is true:

-   Potential impact exceeds **\$25,000**.
-   Critical-path impact could exceed **2 working days**.
-   Owner approval is contractually required.
-   The issue creates safety, code, permit, or commissioning exposure.
-   The action consumes project contingency.
-   Multiple trades are affected and responsibility is disputed.
-   A decision remains unresolved beyond its required-by date.

### Recovery Governance

**Daily:** Superintendent reviews production, constraints, safety,
manpower, and next-day work.\
**Twice Weekly:** PM and Superintendent review critical/near-critical
activities.\
**Weekly:** CPM forecast is updated and compared with the prior
forecast.\
**Monthly:** Executive status report records schedule health, milestone
forecast, and decisions required.

### Recovery Success Criteria

The recovery plan is considered effective when:

-   Weather-tight milestone variance stops increasing.
-   Area A MEP rough-in reaches inspection-ready status.
-   Interior framing starts without uncontrolled moisture or rework
    exposure.
-   No recovery activity creates a recordable safety event.
-   Recovery spending remains within approved management authority.
-   Final completion forecast does not deteriorate.

## 6. Cost & Contingency Management Strategy

The project should not be managed solely against invoices paid. The PM
tracks:

**Original Budget → Approved Changes → Revised Budget → Commitments →
Actual Cost → ETC → Forecast at Completion → Variance**

Current management position:

-   **Baseline Budget:** \$20.600M
-   **Revised Budget:** approximately \$21.057M
-   **Forecast at Completion:** approximately \$20.550M
-   **Forecast Headroom:** approximately \$507K versus revised budget
-   **Actual Cost to Date:** approximately \$10.215M

Forecast headroom is not automatically treated as available savings.
Remaining exposure includes unresolved changes, schedule recovery,
long-lead procurement, commissioning, closeout, and potential owner
scope.

### Contingency Rules

1.  Do not use contingency to hide base-scope estimating errors.
2.  Every contingency draw must have a documented cause.
3.  Track approved, pending, and potential exposure separately.
4.  Require pricing support before approving material changes.
5.  Challenge owner-requested elective scope that threatens project
    objectives.
6.  Update forecast at completion when new information becomes
    probable---not only when invoices arrive.
7.  Review contingency burn rate monthly.

## 7. Change Management Workflow

**Potential Change Identified**\
↓\
**Document Cause / Scope / Responsible Party**\
↓\
**Determine Contract Entitlement**\
↓\
**Obtain Trade Pricing**\
↓\
**PM Performs Scope & Cost Validation**\
↓\
**Assess Schedule Impact**\
↓\
**Negotiate / Clarify**\
↓\
**Owner Approval if Required**\
↓\
**Execute Change Order**\
↓\
**Update Budget + Forecast + Schedule + Logs**

### Change Review Questions

Before recommending approval, the PM asks:

-   Is the work truly outside the contracted scope?
-   What drawing, specification, RFI, directive, or owner request
    created it?
-   Is labor productivity reasonable?
-   Are material quantities supported?
-   Are markups consistent with the contract?
-   Is there duplicate scope between trades?
-   Does the change affect critical-path work?
-   Can the impact be mitigated through resequencing?
-   What contingency or owner funding source will cover it?
-   Does the revised forecast remain acceptable?

## 9. Procurement Recovery & Long-Lead Control

### Main Electrical Gear Watch Plan

The electrical gear remains a critical procurement item because
downstream startup and commissioning depend on permanent power
availability.

**PM controls:**

-   Weekly manufacturer status call.
-   Written confirmation of fabrication status.
-   Approved submittal status verified.
-   Release date documented.
-   Factory completion date documented.
-   Shipping method and transit duration confirmed.
-   Site receiving/storage plan confirmed.
-   Rigging and installation sequence coordinated.
-   Expedite decision threshold established.
-   Temporary-power contingency considered if risk increases.

### Procurement Escalation Thresholds

**GREEN:** Required-on-site date protected by \>14 days.\
**AMBER:** Float reduced to 7--14 days or vendor evidence is
incomplete.\
**RED:** Forecast delivery threatens installation/commissioning
milestone or float \<7 days.

## 10. Safety During Schedule Recovery

Acceleration does not justify unsafe work.

Additional recovery controls include:

-   Pre-task plans for changed sequences.
-   Daily access and housekeeping review.
-   Scaffold inspection before use.
-   Fall-protection verification.
-   Crane/lift planning for equipment setting.
-   Trade stacking review.
-   Overtime fatigue monitoring.
-   Clear pedestrian/equipment separation.
-   Daily superintendent field walk.
-   Immediate correction of high-risk observations.

**Project safety objective:** Maintain **zero recordable incidents**
through completion.

## 12. Executive Escalation Dashboard

### RED --- Immediate Executive Attention

-   Critical-path milestone forecast deteriorates by \>5 working days.
-   Forecast exceeds revised budget.
-   Safety event with serious potential or recordable injury.
-   Long-lead item threatens commissioning or occupancy.
-   Major owner decision is overdue and blocking work.
-   Code/permit issue threatens occupancy.

### AMBER --- Active PM Management

-   Critical activity behind but recovery plan exists.
-   Contingency consumption accelerating.
-   RFI/submittal approaching field need date.
-   Procurement float falls below 14 days.
-   QA/QC deficiency affects downstream work.
-   Repeated subcontractor production miss.

### GREEN --- Controlled

-   Milestones tracking within accepted tolerance.
-   Forecast remains within authorized budget.
-   Procurement dates protected.
-   Safety observations closed promptly.
-   Quality issues corrected before concealment.
-   Closeout deliverables progressing to plan.

## 14. Closeout Readiness Strategy

Closeout begins before construction is physically complete.

### 90 Days Before Turnover

-   Confirm closeout responsibility matrix.
-   Review O&M manual requirements.
-   Begin as-built drawing audits.
-   Confirm commissioning schedule.
-   Validate owner training requirements.
-   Track attic stock/spares.
-   Review warranty requirements.

### 60 Days Before Turnover

-   Start area-based punch planning.
-   Verify equipment startup documentation.
-   Collect warranties and certificates.
-   Confirm TAB and functional testing dates.
-   Track permit/AHJ closeout items.
-   Review subcontractor final documentation.

### 30 Days Before Turnover

-   Complete owner training.
-   Drive punch closure.
-   Complete commissioning deficiencies.
-   Finalize O&M manuals.
-   Deliver as-builts.
-   Resolve open commercial items.
-   Prepare Certificate of Occupancy / substantial completion
    documentation.

### Final Completion

-   Final punch closed.
-   Final inspections complete.
-   Owner documentation accepted.
-   Final lien releases/waivers received as applicable.
-   Final changes reconciled.
-   Lessons learned completed.
-   Project archive established.

## 16. Interview Case Study --- STAR Framework

### Situation

At approximately the halfway point of a simulated **75,000 SF, \$20.6M
corporate operations center**, actual progress was approximately **49.9%
versus 51.7% planned**. Envelope work and Area A MEP rough-in were
behind, while long-lead electrical gear remained a potential downstream
commissioning risk.

### Task

As the simulated Construction Project Manager, the objective was to
protect the weather-tight milestone and final turnover while maintaining
budget control, safety, quality, and clear stakeholder communication.

### Action

I reviewed the CPM schedule and focused recovery on critical and
near-critical activities. I established a south elevation recovery crew,
resequenced available roofing work, drove closure of the Grid C7 MEP
coordination issue, established procurement escalation thresholds for
electrical gear, used phased interior releases, tracked changes against
contingency, and incorporated schedule, cost, procurement, risk, safety,
and quality into an executive reporting process.

### Result

The recovery strategy created a structured path to prevent additional
schedule deterioration while the forecast remained within the revised
project budget. The project maintained zero recordable incidents at the
status date, long-lead items remained actively monitored, and decision
ownership was documented through formal controls.

## 19. Final Management Statement

A strong Construction Project Manager does more than maintain logs. The
PM connects **scope, schedule, cost, procurement, design, field
execution, risk, safety, quality, stakeholders, and closeout** into one
decision-making system.

This playbook demonstrates that management layer: identify the variance,
determine the cause, quantify the exposure, establish options, recommend
an action, assign ownership, set a deadline, execute the recovery, and
verify the result.
