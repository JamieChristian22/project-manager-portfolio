# Construction PM Interview Talking Points

## 30-second project explanation
“I built a full project-controls simulation for a 75,000-square-foot ground-up corporate operations center. I managed a baseline schedule, cost forecast, trade procurement, RFIs, submittals, changes, risk, safety, quality, stakeholder actions and closeout in one integrated control workbook. At the status date the project was about 1.8 percentage points behind plan, so I built a recovery approach around weather-tight work, Area A MEP coordination and long-lead electrical gear while keeping the forecast within the revised budget.”

## What makes this project job-ready
- It links schedule, procurement, design decisions and cost instead of keeping isolated trackers.
- It includes real PM concepts: ETC, FAC, variance, long-lead release, RFI turnaround, change entitlement, risk triggers, owner decision due dates, quality hold points and retention-linked closeout.
- It contains management actions and trade-level controls, not just a static dashboard.

## Strong stories to explain
### 1. Schedule recovery
**Problem:** Structure finished late and envelope/MEP work began to threaten weather-tight and interior start milestones.  
**Action:** Flagged the risk, added a second lift at the south elevation, split storefront deliveries, protected dry-in by elevation, and coordinated Area A MEP as a release zone.  
**Result:** The project remained behind plan but the delay was contained to a manageable forecast rather than cascading into commissioning/turnover.

### 2. Long-lead procurement
**Problem:** Main electrical gear carried a long lead and could affect commissioning.  
**Action:** Released the package early, tracked shop approval and required-on-site dates, held weekly factory confirmation, and created an expedite decision gate instead of automatically spending recovery money.  
**Result:** The ship date remained aligned with the schedule and the team retained a contingency option.

### 3. Cost / change control
**Problem:** Owner enhancements and field conditions generated approved changes while contingency tightened.  
**Action:** Separated gross change activity from net budget movement, coded every change, reforecast ETC/FAC, and recommended owner funding for elective scope rather than silently consuming contingency.  
**Result:** Forecast remained below revised budget with visible remaining headroom.

### 4. RFI / coordination
**Problem:** A duct/sprinkler clash at Grid C7 threatened Area A rough-in and downstream enclosure.  
**Action:** Logged the issue, assigned ball-in-court, set a required response date, tied it to an action and quality deficiency, and required reinspection before concealment.  
**Result:** The issue was controlled as a cross-functional field constraint rather than becoming hidden rework.

### 5. Safety / quality
Explain that you used leading indicators (observations and near misses), immediate containment, corrective action and verification. For quality, you used hold points and reinspection before concealment.

## Questions an interviewer may ask
**How did you build the schedule?**  
By WBS and construction logic: preconstruction -> permit/buyout -> site/foundations -> structure -> envelope/MEP -> interiors -> commissioning -> closeout. I used predecessor logic, planned/actual dates, progress and critical flags, then focused management on milestone exposure.

**How did you forecast cost?**  
Revised Budget = Original Budget + approved budget adjustments. FAC = Actual Cost to Date + ETC. Variance = Revised Budget - FAC. I reviewed this by cost code instead of only at total-project level.

**How do you control changes?**  
No change becomes “just a field request.” It gets documented, priced, checked for entitlement/duplication, assessed for schedule impact, coded, approved, then incorporated into the budget/forecast and contract records.

**What would you improve next?**  
I would add a detailed CPM network export, earned-value curves, pay application workflow, subcontractor scorecards, and a BIM clash dashboard if the employer uses those systems.
